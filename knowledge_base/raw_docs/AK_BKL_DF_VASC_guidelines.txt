=== AK: ACTINIC KERATOSIS ===

OVERVIEW
Actinic keratosis (AK) is a pre-malignant lesion caused by cumulative UV damage.
It is the precursor to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Treatment is recommended to
prevent malignant transformation.

CLINICAL FEATURES
- Rough, scaly patch on sun-damaged skin
- Flat to slightly raised patch with dry, scaly surface
- Itching, burning, or tenderness in the affected area
- Hard, wart-like surface; color varies (pink, red, or brown)
- Commonly on face, lips, ears, back of hands, forearms, scalp, neck

MANAGEMENT
- Cryotherapy: liquid nitrogen — most common treatment
- Topical agents: 5-FU, imiquimod, ingenol mebutate for field treatment
- Photodynamic therapy: for multiple/field AKs
- All AKs should be treated — 10% may progress to SCC within 10 years
- Annual skin checks recommended after diagnosis

PATIENT ADVICE
- Strict UV avoidance and SPF 50+ daily
- Monitor and consult a doctor if the lesion changes, grows, bleeds, or becomes painful

=== BKL: BENIGN KERATOSIS-LIKE LESION (Seborrhoeic Keratosis) ===

OVERVIEW
Seborrhoeic keratoses are benign, non-cancerous growths that become more common
with age. They are entirely benign but can be mistaken for melanoma or BCC.

CLINICAL FEATURES
- "Stuck-on" appearance — waxy, warty surface
- Range from light tan to dark brown or black
- Varies in size from a few millimetres to several centimetres
- Multiple lesions common; found on trunk, face, shoulders, back
- NOT caused by sun exposure; NOT pre-malignant

MANAGEMENT
- No treatment required if benign
- Removal for cosmetic reasons or if inflamed/irritated (cryotherapy, curettage)
- Biopsy if diagnosis is uncertain (cannot reliably exclude melanoma clinically)

PATIENT ADVICE
- Lesions are benign — no cancer risk
- Monitor and consult a doctor if any individual lesion changes rapidly, bleeds,
  or becomes asymmetric — these may indicate a co-existing melanoma

=== DF: DERMATOFIBROMA ===

OVERVIEW
Dermatofibromas are benign fibrous nodules, most commonly found on the legs.
They are harmless and require no treatment unless symptomatic or cosmetically bothersome.

CLINICAL FEATURES
- Firm, raised bump, usually 0.5–1.5cm
- Typically pink, red, grey, or brown
- "Dimple sign": pinching the lesion causes it to dimple inward
- Slightly tender to touch; may itch

MANAGEMENT
- No treatment required
- Surgical excision if cosmetically bothersome or symptomatic
- Biopsy if diagnosis uncertain (rare malignant variants: dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans)

PATIENT ADVICE
- Lesion is benign; monitoring sufficient
- Monitor and consult a doctor if it changes, grows rapidly, or becomes painful

=== VASC: VASCULAR LESION ===

OVERVIEW
Vascular lesions include a heterogeneous group: haemangiomas, pyogenic granulomas,
angiokeratomas, cherry angiomas. Most are benign but pyogenic granuloma bleeds
readily and may mimic amelanotic melanoma.

CLINICAL FEATURES
- Pyogenic granuloma: rapidly growing, friable, bleeds easily — RED FLAG for referral
- Cherry angioma: small, bright-red dome; extremely common; entirely benign
- Angiokeratoma: dark red/purple warty papule; may mimic melanoma

MANAGEMENT
- Cherry angioma: no treatment required
- Pyogenic granuloma: excision or shave biopsy — always send for histology
- Any bleeding vascular lesion should be evaluated to exclude amelanotic melanoma

PATIENT ADVICE
- Monitor and consult a doctor if the lesion bleeds, grows rapidly, or changes
- Bleeding lesions require prompt evaluation regardless of apparent cause
